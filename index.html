Local Software Firm VIF-Office Grants Unusual Interview
Employees rarely speak to the press. This week, one did. He asked us not to print his name. We agreed.

RIVERTON — In an industry where most companies speak only through press releases, VIF-Office has said even less. The software firm operates out of a low brick building on McAllister Road, where the parking lot holds nine cars on a busy day. Repeated requests for comment over the past two years have gone unanswered. Last Tuesday, however, a staff member — who agreed to speak only on condition that his name and employee number not be published — telephoned this paper's front desk and asked whether we were still interested.

We were. What follows is a transcript of that forty-five minute conversation, edited only for length and for the removal of several questions the employee declined to answer.

RTB: For readers who have never heard of VIF-Office, what does the company do?

EMPLOYEE: We make tools. Software tools. The kind you don't see in stores. Other companies use our tools to make their own things. I have worked there for some years now and I am still not sure I could explain it to my mother. That is not because it is secret. It is because it is hard.

RTB: What is the atmosphere like inside?

EMPLOYEE: Quiet. Very quiet. The coffee machine has been broken since March. Management put a sign on it that says "The direction we have taken is correct." I asked whether that was supposed to fix the coffee machine. My supervisor said it wasn't about the coffee machine.

RTB: That sign — is it some kind of company motto?

EMPLOYEE: It is painted on the wall in the main hallway. It has been there longer than most of us. The paint is old, the letters are starting to fade. Nobody questions it. I tried asking once, a few years ago. My supervisor said the sentence was a decision that was made before the decision makers. I think about that a lot.

RTB: What product is the company most proud of?

EMPLOYEE: There is an editor. Not the kind you write letters in. It handles shapes. Models. Wireframes. The senior people just call it "the editor," and everyone knows which one they mean. Sometimes the machines complain that there are too many surfaces. The error message is very polite about it. I have always found that strange. A machine that apologizes for being full.

RTB: Is the company prepared for the year 2000? A lot of our readers are asking that question about local businesses.

EMPLOYEE: We had a meeting about it. Someone asked whether the systems would survive the year 2000. Management said the direction we have taken is correct. That is the answer to a lot of things, I have noticed. Budgets, schedules, coffee machines. I think it is the only sentence in the building that never needs updating.

RTB: What do you remember most about working there?

EMPLOYEE: The old building, before we moved. It was out on Route 89, past the grain silo. Smaller place. Fewer of us. There was a staircase that went down to a room that wasn't on any floor plan. They kept it locked. I asked about it once. Management said the direction we have taken is correct. I stopped asking.

RTB: Is there anything the public should know about the company?

EMPLOYEE: Only that it is still there. That is more than can be said for a lot of firms this decade. The lights are on. The work continues. Whatever the work is.

The employee requested that the interview end after forty-five minutes. It did. VIF-Office declined to provide an official comment for this article. Through the staff member, the company relayed only that it "continues its work."

A videotape of the interview was recorded and will be made available online once conversion from the office's Hi8 camera is complete.
